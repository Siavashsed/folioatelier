Your Savings Rate Matters Less Than Your Defaults

General finance tips that work because they remove decisions. Automation, a cash buffer and one monthly money check beat willpower every time.

The best general finance tip is also the dullest: stop relying on your own discipline and automate every decision you can. In March 2022 I sat in my kitchen at 11pm, wrestling a spreadsheet with 14 budget categories, and I abandoned it by April. What actually moved my net worth was one standing order of $250 on the 2nd of each month, set up in about four minutes.

Most money advice assumes you'll make a good choice every single month. You won't, and neither will I. The people I've worked with on personal budgets, many of them freelance illustrators and collective members with lumpy income, almost never fail from ignorance. They fail because the plan needs willpower on a bad Tuesday.

Why a cash buffer comes before any clever strategy

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found that in 2023 only 63% of U.S. adults could cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. That leaves more than a third of people one car repair away from a credit card.

So before you open a brokerage account, build a buffer. For salaried people, one month of expenses is a fine first target. If you earn irregularly, I'd push it to three. I learned that the hard way when a gallery client paid 74 days late and I covered rent with a card at 24% interest.

Keep the buffer boring. A high-yield savings account at a separate bank, with no debit card attached, works because the friction is the point.

Automation beats motivation, and there's data on it

Economists Shlomo Benartzi and Richard Thaler tested this in their Save More Tomorrow study, published in 2004. Employees who agreed to route future raises into savings pushed their average rate from 3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months. Nobody felt a pay cut, because the money never showed up in their hands.

What to automate first

  • A transfer to savings the day after payday
  • Retirement contributions, raised by 1% each time you get a raise
  • Minimum payments on every debt, with the extra sent to the highest-rate one
  • A monthly sinking fund for annual bills like insurance and software

I've written before about why finance habits stick or fade, and the pattern is the same each time. Anything that needs a decision gets skipped.

The tracking trap, and where I stand on budgeting

Here's an unpopular view. Tracking every coffee is a poor use of your attention. Detailed budgets feel responsible, but in my experience they collapse within two months for most people, then leave guilt behind.

I use three buckets instead: fixed costs, savings, and everything else. If the first two are covered automatically, the third can be spent without a second thought. It isn't perfect. People with very tight margins or heavy debt may genuinely need line-by-line tracking, and I wouldn't tell them otherwise.

Honestly, I'm not 100% sure the three-bucket method works for everyone. My friend Dana tried it and blew through her "everything else" money in nine days on a ceramics kiln she'd wanted since 2019. She says she regrets nothing. I bought a $90 label maker and used it twice, so I'm hardly one to judge... but it did teach us both that the bucket needs a ceiling you can live with.

One monthly money check is enough

You don't need a daily dashboard. Set a 20-minute appointment with yourself on the same day each month. Check balances, confirm the transfers ran, and look for any subscription you forgot about.

That last one pays off more than people expect. When I audited my own accounts I found $43 a month in services I hadn't opened in a year. If you want a longer list of mistakes to dodge, my piece on money lessons people pick up too expensively covers the usual suspects, and the one on changing your financial life, not just your spreadsheet goes further on mindset.

Small, repeated, dull. That's the whole system.

Open your bank app right now and schedule one automatic transfer for the day after your next payday. Pick an amount that feels almost too small, even $25, and raise it by $10 next month.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first thing I should do with my money?

Build a small cash buffer, ideally one month of expenses, in a separate savings account. Then automate a monthly transfer so saving doesn't depend on your mood.

How much should I save each month?

A common starting point is 10% to 15% of income, but starting at 3% and raising it with each pay rise works better than quitting a too-ambitious plan. The habit matters more than the first number.

Do I really need a detailed budget?

Not always. Many people do better with a simple split of fixed costs, automatic savings and free spending. Detailed tracking is worth it if you carry high-interest debt or have very little margin.
