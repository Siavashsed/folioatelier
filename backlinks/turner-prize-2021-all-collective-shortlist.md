**Title:** Was the 2021 all-collective Turner Prize shortlist a one-off, or the moment the prize admitted where art had already moved?

I've been going back over the 2021 Turner Prize, and I think it gets dismissed too easily as a pandemic-year oddity. If you missed it: the jury shortlisted five collectives and no individual artists. They were Array Collective (Belfast), Black Obsidian Sound System, Cooking Sections, Gentle/Radical (Cardiff) and Project Art Works (Hastings). Array won on 1 December with The Druthaib's Ball, a recreated sibin (an illicit drinking den) built inside the gallery in Coventry.

What stands out to me is that the jury wasn't really rewarding objects. It was rewarding ways of working: shared authorship, long-term relationships with communities, and organising around disability, land, food, and queer and trans communities of colour. Plenty of critics called it the year the prize stopped being about art. I'd say it was the year the prize caught up with where a lot of the most interesting practice already was.

The practical problems haven't gone away, though. A £25,000 prize split across a whole collective is a very different thing from a solo award, and gallery shows still struggle to present process.

Did it shift anything long term, or was it a single gesture?

## LINKEDIN
**Hook:** Britain's best-known art prize once shortlisted zero individual artists. That was the whole point.

In May 2021, for the first time, the Turner Prize jury shortlisted five collectives and not a single solo artist: Array Collective, Black Obsidian Sound System, Cooking Sections, Gentle/Radical and Project Art Works. Array took the prize on 1 December in Coventry.

The lesson reaches beyond the art world. The jury rewarded practices built on shared authorship, long-term trust with communities, and work that carries on after the exhibition closes. Those are structures, not just outputs.

It also showed a gap. Prize money, gallery formats and credit systems are still built around one name on the wall. When many people make the work, institutions have to change how they count it, pay for it and present it.

Any organisation that runs on collaboration but rewards individuals faces the same question.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why was the 2021 Turner Prize shortlist made up entirely of art collectives?

Short answer: the jury chose to reward how art was actually being made in 2020 and 2021, and much of the strongest work in Britain then was made by groups rather than individuals.

The five shortlisted were Array Collective from Belfast, Black Obsidian Sound System (B.O.S.S.), Cooking Sections, Gentle/Radical from Cardiff, and Project Art Works from Hastings. Array won on 1 December 2021, with the exhibition staged in Coventry.

Three forces came together.

First, the pandemic. Galleries were closed for long stretches, so the jury looked at practices that kept working with people anyway: community kitchens, neighbourhood projects, mutual support, gatherings that moved online. Collectives were built for exactly that.

Second, the subject matter. Each group works on something bigger than one career. Array works on activism in Northern Ireland, including reproductive and LGBTQ+ rights. B.O.S.S. is a sound system and platform for queer, trans and non-binary artists of colour. Cooking Sections examines food systems and climate through its Climavore project. Gentle/Radical works on culture and faith in a Cardiff neighbourhood. Project Art Works collaborates with neurodivergent artists and their families.

Third, a longer shift already under way. In 2019 the four Turner nominees asked to be awarded the prize jointly, and the same year the Indonesian collective ruangrupa was named artistic director of documenta fifteen. 2021 made that shift official.

Reaction was split. Some critics argued the prize had stopped judging art and started judging social programmes. Others saw it as an overdue correction to the myth of the lone genius.
