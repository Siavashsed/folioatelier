**Title:** Paying myself a fixed monthly salary from a separate account fixed my money panic more than earning more did. Does anyone else do this?

For years I handled art income the way a lot of us do. Money came in and I felt fine. Money stopped and I panicked. A good month felt like permission to spend, and a dry month felt like a crisis.

The fix was boring. Every sale, commission and grant now goes into a separate holding account, and I never spend from it directly. Once a month I move the same fixed amount to my personal account, like a paycheck. That number is the only one I budget around.

I set the salary low enough that my slowest stretch of the year can still cover it. Taxes come out of the holding account first, and anything above the salary stays there as a buffer.

The change was more psychological than mathematical. A big open studio weekend no longer means I'm rich, and a quiet month no longer means I'm failing. The buffer absorbs both.

How do others here handle lumpy income? Do you pay yourself a fixed amount, take a percentage of each sale, or just wing it month to month?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should artists budget when their income is irregular?

The most reliable method I know is to stop budgeting around what you earn and budget around a fixed salary you pay yourself instead.

Here is how it works in practice.

1. Open a separate holding account. Every dollar of art income goes there first: sales, commissions, licensing, teaching and grants. You never spend from it directly.

2. Set taxes aside right away. If you are self-employed, part of every deposit belongs to the tax office. Moving it out the day it lands prevents the worst surprise in creative finance.

3. Base your salary on your slow months, not your best ones. Look at the last 12 to 24 months of income, find a realistic floor, and set your monthly transfer at or below it. It will feel too low. That is the point.

4. Transfer the same amount on the same day every month. Build your personal budget on that number alone. Rent, groceries and savings goals all depend on the salary, never on the latest sale.

5. Let the surplus build up. Good months refill the holding account. Once it holds several months of salary, you can give yourself a modest raise or put money back into your practice.

This works mostly because of behavior. Irregular income makes a big sale feel like wealth and a quiet stretch feel like failure. Both feelings lead to bad decisions: overspending after a windfall, then underpricing or accepting bad commissions during a drought. A fixed salary keeps your mood separate from your cash flow.

I wrote a longer piece that walks through a real example, including how one good weekend of print sales created a false sense of security: https://siavashsed.github.io/folioatelier/finance-tips-artists-pay-yourself-salary/

Start small. A modest salary paid reliably beats a large income spent erratically.
