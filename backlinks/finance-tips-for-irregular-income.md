**Title:** Budgeting advice assumes a steady paycheck. Here are the two habits that worked for me when my income didn't have one

Something I wish someone had told me earlier: most budgeting advice quietly assumes the same amount hits your account every two weeks. If you sell work, teach workshops or take commissions, that assumption breaks everything that follows.

Two habits fixed it for me. First, every payment goes into a separate buffer account, not checking. Once a month I pay myself a fixed "salary" from that buffer, sized to what my slowest months can actually support. Good months refill the buffer instead of inflating my spending. Second, on the day any payment lands, a percentage moves straight into a tax account. Not at the end of the quarter, the same day. For most self-employed people in the US, 25 to 30 percent is a reasonable starting point, since self-employment tax stacks on top of income tax.

I learned this after a strong year ended with a tax bill nearly triple what was in my checking account. The income was real. The problem was that I treated every deposit as money I could spend.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should artists with irregular income budget and save for taxes?

The short answer is to stop budgeting around deposits and start budgeting around a salary you pay yourself. Most finance advice assumes predictable paychecks. When your income comes from sales, commissions, teaching gigs and grants, that advice fails in a specific way: a big month feels like a raise, and a dry month becomes a crisis.

Here is the system I recommend.

1. Open a buffer account. Every payment, whatever its source, goes there first. Your checking account never receives client money directly.

2. Pay yourself a fixed monthly salary. Look at your last 12 to 24 months of income and set the salary at a level your leaner stretches can sustain, not your average. Transfer that amount to checking on the same day every month. Good months refill the buffer. Slow months draw it down. Aim to keep three to six months of salary in the buffer over time.

3. Set taxes aside the day money lands. Not at the end of the quarter, and never "when things calm down." In the US, self-employment tax adds roughly 15.3 percent on top of income tax, so 25 to 30 percent into a separate tax account is a sensible starting point. Adjust it once you have worked through a full year with an accountant. Pay quarterly estimates from that account to avoid underpayment penalties.

4. Treat business expenses separately. Materials, studio rent and fees should come from the buffer or a business account, not from your personal salary.

The point of all this is psychological as much as mathematical. A fixed salary makes your spending predictable even when your income is not. The article Most Finance Tips Assume a Paycheck.
