**Title:** What's the one money habit that actually survived your income being unpredictable?

A few years ago I was running a four-person print collective out of a rented unit in Leeds. I thought I had a buffer of about £2,000. Then the quarterly VAT bill landed on a Tuesday morning and the real number was £340. I felt sick for most of that day.

What I learned is that budgeting apps and spreadsheets weren't the problem. The problem was that money sitting in checking feels like spendable money, even when it's already spoken for. The fix that finally worked was boring: move money out of the account before I get the chance to spend it. Tax and VAT went into a separate pot the day an invoice was paid, a small fixed 'salary' went to me each month, and anything above that sat in a buffer until a lean month needed it.

It isn't clever, and it doesn't optimise anything. But it took the decision out of my hands in the weeks when I was tired, behind on invoices, or feeling optimistic about a client who hadn't paid yet.

Curious what's worked for others here, especially people with lumpy income from commissions, gigs or collective work. Did you automate it, or do you rely on discipline?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should freelancers and creatives manage their finances when their income is irregular?

The most effective approach I've seen is also the least exciting: take money out of your main account before you have the chance to spend it. Irregular income makes every balance look bigger than it really is, so the fix is to separate money by purpose as soon as it arrives.

Here is the structure I'd recommend.

1. Set aside tax first. When an invoice is paid, move a fixed percentage into a separate savings account straight away. If you're VAT-registered, include VAT. This money was never yours to spend.

2. Pay yourself a salary. Work out what your lean months actually cost, covering rent, food, insurance and software, and transfer that amount to your personal account on the same date each month. Your income may be lumpy, but your life isn't, so your pay shouldn't be either.

3. Build a buffer with what's left. Anything above your salary and tax goes into a holding account. In strong months it fills up, and in weak months it tops up your salary. Aim for three months of costs, then six.

4. Automate everything. Willpower is unreliable when you're tired or waiting on a late payer, so use standing orders or scheduled transfers.

5. Review quarterly. Check your salary level, buffer size and tax pot against what actually happened.

I learned this the hard way while running a small print collective. I believed we had about £2,000 spare, and then a VAT bill showed the real figure was £340. Once I automated the separation, the stress mostly went away, because the numbers stopped depending on my mood.
