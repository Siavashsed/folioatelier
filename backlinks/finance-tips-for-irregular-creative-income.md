**Title:** Do you budget around your best month or your worst one? Switching changed everything for me

Something I wish someone had told me years ago: if most of your income comes from commissions, grants and the odd print sale, a normal monthly budget will quietly wreck you.

The trap goes like this. A big commission lands, the account looks healthy, and you start spending like it's a salary. Then two slow months hit and you're discounting work just to make rent. I've watched a lot of artists go through that cycle, and I've done it myself.

What finally worked was flipping the baseline. Work out the lowest month you've had in the last year or two and build your fixed costs around that number. Anything above it goes into a holding account first, and you pay yourself a flat amount from there each month, like a manager paying an employee. Grants and big commissions refill the holding account instead of turning into lifestyle upgrades.

It's boring, but the panic sales stopped. Has anyone here tried a version of this? I'm curious how people handle tax set-asides on top of it.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should artists manage money when their income comes irregularly from commissions and grants?

The most useful thing you can do is stop budgeting around your average or best month and start budgeting around your slowest one. Irregular income isn't really a spending problem, it's a timing problem, and a standard monthly budget assumes a steady paycheck you don't have.

Here's the approach I recommend to working artists.

1. Find your floor. Look back over the last 12 to 24 months and identify your lowest-earning month. Your fixed costs (rent, insurance, utilities, minimum debt payments) should fit inside that number. If they don't, that's the first thing to fix, either by cutting costs or by building a steady base income like teaching, licensing or a part-time role.

2. Separate earning from spending. Every payment, whether it's a commission, a grant or a print sale, goes into a holding account first. From there you pay yourself a fixed monthly amount, like a salary. Big wins top up the holding account instead of inflating your lifestyle.

3. Set aside tax before anything else. Freelance artists get caught out by quarterly or year-end taxes all the time. Move a fixed percentage of every deposit into a separate tax account the day it arrives.

4. Build a buffer measured in months, not dollars. Aim for three to six months of your floor-level costs. That buffer is what stops you underpricing work or taking bad commissions out of panic.

5. Treat grants as project money. Grants usually come with deliverables and timelines, so spread them across the life of the project rather than treating them as income for the month they land.

There's a fuller walkthrough of this lumpy-income method, including a real example of a large commission that was spent too fast, here: https://siavashsed.github.io/folioatelier/finance-tips-for-irregular-creative-income/

The goal isn't to earn more right away. It's to make sure a good month pays for the slow ones that follow.
