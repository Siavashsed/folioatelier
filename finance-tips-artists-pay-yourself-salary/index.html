Artists Who Pay Themselves a Salary Stop Panicking About Money

General finance tips for artists and creative collectives with irregular income: a holding account, a fixed salary, tax set-asides and written money rules.

If your income comes from art, the most useful general finance tip I know is this: stop budgeting around what you earn and budget around a fixed salary you pay yourself from a holding account. In April 2019 I sold four large prints at a Philadelphia open studio weekend for $2,140. I felt rich for about nine days. Then, at 1 a.m. in early June, I sat at my kitchen table with a cold mug of rooibos and worked out that the June 15 estimated tax payment had already eaten all of it. That night changed how I run my studio money, and it's why I think most standard money advice fails people who make things for a living.

Most budgeting guides assume a paycheck that lands on the same day every two weeks. Artists don't get that. You get a $3,000 commission in October, nothing in November, and three $80 Etsy sales in December. And the stakes are real. The Federal Reserve's 2024 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found that only 63% of adults would cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or something like it. For someone whose income swings month to month, that cushion matters even more. What follows is the system I use myself and have set up with the collectives I work with.

Pay Yourself a Salary From a Holding Account

How the holding account works

Every dollar from sales, grants, teaching and commissions lands in one high-yield savings account. Nothing gets spent straight from it. On the first of each month, a fixed amount moves from that account into your checking account. That transfer is your salary. It's the same number whether you sold a painting last week or haven't sold one since August.

It sounds rigid. It is. But the rigidity is the point, because it puts a gap between a good month and a spending spree. Your spending stops tracking your sales. When I set this up in 2020, my salary was $2,600 a month, and it took around five months of saving before the holding account had enough in it to make that feel safe.

Picking the number

Add up your last 12 months of income after tax and business costs, then divide by 12. Take about 80% of that as your salary. The other 20% stays in the account and builds up during good stretches, so it can cover the lean ones. If the balance grows past six months of salary for two quarters in a row, give yourself a raise. If it drops below three months, cut your salary before the account cuts it for you.

Tax Money Isn't Yours, So Move It the Day It Arrives

Self-employed artists in the US pay a 15.3% self-employment tax for Social Security and Medicare, on top of regular income tax. Nobody withholds it for you. The IRS expects quarterly estimated payments if you'll owe $1,000 or more, and underpaying adds penalties on top.

So I keep a separate savings account called TAXES, at a different bank from my everyday account so it's a little annoying to reach. When money hits the holding account, a set share goes straight there:

  • 25% for most working artists earning under roughly $60,000 a year
  • 30% if you earn more, or if your state income tax is high
  • Whatever's left over in April becomes a small bonus, which beats a scary bill

I've seen this pattern dozens of times: an artist has a breakout year, spends like they had one, and spends the next year paying it back to the IRS. A good year with no tax set-aside often does more damage than a bad year. For more mistakes like this one, I wrote up the finance tips most people learn too late and too expensively.

Collectives Need Written Money Rules Before They Need Money

In my work with a twelve-member printmaking collective in 2022, the biggest fight in four years was over $1,800 and a broken etching press. Nobody had written down who paid for repairs, what dues were for, or what happened to a member's share when they left. People meant well. The fight happened anyway.

If you share a space, a kiln or a gallery wall, write a one-page agreement covering dues, a repair fund, how big purchases get approved, and how exits work. Keep the collective's money in its own account with two signers. It's dull paperwork... and it's the stuff that keeps friendships intact.

My friend Dana once ran her ceramics group's money out of a Nike shoebox with envelopes labeled in Sharpie. Honestly, it worked for about a year, until her cat knocked it off a shelf and the "glaze fund" envelope ended up under the radiator for three weeks. I'm not 100% sure cash envelopes are wrong for a tiny group, either. Some people really do spend less when they can see the money. But please don't use a shoebox.

Where Standard Advice Breaks for Creative Work

The emergency fund debate

The usual line is to save three to six months of expenses. For artists, I'd argue for six to nine, kept separate from your holding account. Some planners disagree and say paying off high-interest debt should come first, and they have a point if you're carrying a card at 24% APR. My position: build one month of cushion, kill the expensive debt, then keep building. Doing it in that order has worked for most artists I've seen try it, at least in my experience.

Retirement is not optional just because nobody offers a 401(k)

A SEP IRA or a Solo 401(k) lets self-employed people save far more each year than a standard IRA. The common misconception is that you need to be earning a lot first. You don't. Even $100 a month from your salary account counts. Automate it the day after your salary lands, so you never see it. The habits side of this is covered in finance tips that actually stick, and if you want the bigger-picture version, read finance tips that change your financial life, not just your spreadsheet.

None of this makes irregular income comfortable. It makes it predictable, and for me that turned out to be enough.

Do this today: open a second savings account, name it TAXES, and move 25% of the last payment you received into it before you spend anything else. Tomorrow you can work out your salary number. The tax account comes first, because that's the bill that will find you either way.

Frequently asked questions

How much should an artist set aside for taxes?

Most self-employed artists in the US should set aside 25% of each payment, or 30% if they earn more or live in a high-tax state. That covers the 15.3% self-employment tax plus federal and state income tax for most people. Put it in a separate account the day the money arrives.

How do I budget when my art income is different every month?

Send all income into one holding account and pay yourself the same salary from it every month. Set the salary at about 80% of your average monthly income from the past year after tax and costs. Good months fill the account and lean months draw it down.

Should an art collective have its own bank account?

Yes. A separate account with two signers, plus a one-page written agreement on dues, repairs and member exits, prevents most money disputes. Mixing collective funds with one member's personal account is how small disagreements turn into big ones.
