The $1,870 Tax Bill That Rewired How I Handle Money

General finance tips for artists and collectives on irregular income: tax set-asides, month-based buffers, shared accounts and what to fund first.

The best general finance tip I know for anyone on irregular income is a boring one. Decide where every payment goes before it arrives, and move the tax slice out the same day. I learned that the expensive way. In March 2021 a brewery in Asheville paid me $6,200 for a mural. I treated it like a windfall, and the next April I owed the IRS $1,870 that I'd already spent on a used kiln and two months of rent at a shared studio. Most money advice assumes you get a salary. If you make work for a living, you need rules built for lumpy income, and you need fewer of them than the blogs suggest.

For the past six years I've kept the books for a small print collective with nine members, and the same pattern keeps coming back. People rarely go broke from one bad decision. They go broke from good months that felt bigger than they were. Irregular income punishes optimism more than it punishes spending. So what follows is about structure rather than willpower, because willpower is gone by the third week of a slow month.

Pay the Tax Account Before You Pay Yourself

Here's the misconception I hear most from artists: a client paid me on a 1099, so the tax is handled. It isn't. Nobody withheld anything. On top of regular income tax you owe self-employment tax, which the IRS sets at 15.3% for Social Security and Medicare. That's the part that caught me in 2022.

What I do now is move 28% of every gross payment into a separate savings account at a different bank within 24 hours of it landing. The other bank matters. If the money sits one tap away in my banking app, I'll "borrow" it for framing or a residency fee and tell myself I'll put it back. From that account I pay quarterly estimates using Form 1040-ES, which also keeps me clear of underpayment penalties.

The trade-off is real. In a lean year 28% overshoots and you're sitting on cash you could have used. I'd still rather get a refund than open a letter in April. Your rate depends on your state and your deductions, so run it once with an accountant and then stop thinking about it.

Measure Your Emergency Fund in Months, Not Dollars

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found that in 2023 only 63% of U.S. adults would cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. Among working artists I'd guess that number is lower. But $400 is the wrong yardstick for you anyway. A broken kiln, a cancelled commission or a gallery that pays 90 days late isn't a $400 problem.

Work out your monthly floor instead. Mine came to $2,340 in 2023. Only count what you can't skip:

  • Rent and studio space
  • Health insurance premiums
  • Groceries at a plain, unexciting level
  • Minimum payments on any debt
  • Phone and the software subscriptions you actually invoice with

Aim for three months of that floor first, then six. Once you have six, a dead quarter stops being a crisis. For more on this, see The $400 Rule I Wish Someone Had Told Me About Money at 25.

Pay yourself a fake salary

Every payment goes into one business account. On the first of the month I pay myself the same fixed amount, no matter what came in. Big months refill the account and slow months drain it. It sounds like an accounting trick, and it is one... and it's the single habit that stopped my mood from following my bank balance.

Where Collectives Get Money Wrong

Collectives run into a problem individual artists don't: shared money with fuzzy ownership. I've watched two groups break up, and neither one fell apart over art. One broke over $3,400 in unpaid dues and the other over a joint account only one member could sign on. Put the money rules in writing before anyone is angry. Who can spend, how much without a vote, what happens to the balance if someone leaves. A one-page agreement does more than an hour of goodwill.

My friend Tomasz ran his collective's petty cash out of a Tupperware box in the studio freezer for about two years. His reasoning was that nobody steals frozen peas. Honestly, it worked better than our spreadsheet did for a while, right up until someone defrosted the freezer and the box got tossed with a bag of freezer-burnt edamame. Around $260 went in the dumpster. I'm not 100% sure a proper system would've saved them, because they were bad at logging receipts either way, but now they use a free business checking account with two signers and it's fine. Mostly fine.

So keep the shared account separate from everyone's personal money, reconcile it monthly in a meeting, and rotate the person who does it. A lot of the mistakes in finance tips most people learn too late show up in group money first, because nobody feels it's their job.

Debt, Retirement and the Advice I'd Argue With

Popular advice says to throw every spare dollar at high-interest debt right away. If you're salaried, I agree. If your income swings, I don't, at least not until you have one month of floor saved. In my work with the collective I've seen people wipe out a credit card in a good month, hit a dry spell, and run the balance back up at 24% because they had no cash. A small buffer first, then aggressive payoff. It's slower on paper and it holds up better in real life.

Retirement is where artists put things off the longest. There's no employer match coming. A SEP IRA lets you contribute roughly 20% of net self-employment earnings, and you can decide the amount after the year ends. That flexibility suits lumpy income. I put in nothing in 2020 and a decent chunk in 2023, and the account didn't care.

There's real debate about avalanche versus snowball repayment, and I'm not going to pretend it's settled. The math favors avalanche. Behavior often favors snowball. Pick the one you'll actually keep doing, which is the whole argument behind finance tips that actually stick. And if you want the bigger picture of what changes once the systems run on their own, I wrote about that in finance tips that change your financial life, not just your spreadsheet. For more on this, see The Money Rules Nobody Told Me Until I'd Wasted Three Years.

Do this today. Open a savings account at a bank you don't use for anything else, name it "Tax," and move 25 to 30% of the last payment you received into it. Then set a calendar reminder for the next quarterly estimate date. That one account would have saved me $1,870 and a very bad April.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a freelance artist set aside for taxes?

Most self-employed artists in the U.S. should set aside 25 to 30% of each gross payment. That covers the 15.3% self-employment tax plus federal and state income tax. Confirm your exact rate with an accountant once, then automate it.

How big should my emergency fund be if my income is irregular?

Work it out in months of essential costs, not a flat dollar figure. Start with three months of your baseline (rent, studio, insurance, food, minimum debt payments) and build toward six.

Should an art collective have its own bank account?

Yes. Use a separate business account with at least two signers, and keep a short written agreement on spending limits and member exits. Mixing group money with personal accounts is the fastest way to lose both the money and the friendships.
